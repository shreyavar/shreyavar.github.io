Quantum information dynamics in chaotic systems and black holes

The ubiquitous process of thermalization poses some of the deepest puzzles in nature. When an ice cube melts or milk spreads in a cup of coffee, the macroscopic process that we observe is remarkably different from the underlying microscopic dynamics. From the macroscopic perspective, detailed information about the initial state seems to be lost from the system, which evolves to a predictable equilibrium state. Microscopically, information is never truly lost due to unitarity. Where does the information go, and what are the universal features of its dynamics across generic chaotic quantum many-body systems? The last two decades have seen fruitful explorations into this question, but many fundamental aspects of quantum information dynamics still remain to be understood.

In quantum gravity, a central unresolved puzzle since the 1970s is Hawking's black hole information loss paradox. The paradox arises from the fact that according to semiclassical gravity, the process of black hole evaporation destroys quantum information and violates the unitarity of quantum mechanics. Over the last few decades, it has been understood that the true quantum description of a black hole must obey unitarity. However, understanding how and why the semiclassical description of a black hole breaks down, and how quantum information evolves in its true quantum description, remain pressing open questions.

Black holes can be viewed as examples of chaotic quantum many-body systems through holographic duality, so that the key questions above are closely related to each other:

What are the universal features of quantum information dynamics in chaotic systems?

↔

How do black holes evolve in their true quantum description?

My research is focused on addressing the above questions in the fields of quantum many-body physics, quantum gravity, and quantum information theory. In my work, I often view quantum dynamics in terms of how they naturally process quantum information, or how one can process the information in the states or operators produced by them. Throughout my research, I have found that this approach illuminates novel and surprising universal structures shared across large classes of chaotic quantum many-body systems (see [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8] for examples). My work has also shown that these ideas from quantum chaos and quantum information can help address long-standing puzzles about black holes, as well as raise intriguing new problems (see [9, 10, 1, 3, 4, 5]). To address these questions, my research makes use of diverse theoretical techniques, ranging from random quantum circuits and free probability to quantum field theory and gravitational path integrals.

My recent work also intersects with quantum computing. Many current quantum computing platforms can be seen as examples of generic, chaotic quantum many-body systems. Hence, ideas drawn from quantum many-body chaos can make pivotal new contributions to classical and quantum algorithms for simulating such systems. Experimental data from quantum simulations can also greatly enrich our understanding of quantum chaos, quantum information dynamics, and quantum gravity. This motivates the third key direction of my research program:

Can we use our understanding of quantum chaos to develop algorithms for simulating quantum many-body systems?

See [11] for a recent work where we developed an algorithm to classically simulate out-of-time-ordered correlators in random quantum circuits using insights from quantum chaos.
